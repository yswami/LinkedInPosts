Day 2/5 — A fixed 5% threshold is not a financial-alert strategy

At 8:20, Project Meridian’s financial data has been reconciled.

Approved cost budget: $80M
Validated EAC: $88M
Validated variance: +$8M, or +10%

A simple rule now says:

“Variance exceeds 5%. Send a red alert.”

But a threshold knows the size of the variance—not its decision context.

Consider two versions of the same +10% signal.

Context A:
• $8.5M of policy-recognized contingency remains
• This is the first forecast cycle showing the variance
• The team has 12 months to respond
• No critical commitment is immediately affected

Context B:
• Only $1M of contingency remains
• The variance has persisted for three cycles
• Two months remain in the decision window
• A contractual commitment may be affected

The percentage is identical. The appropriate interruption is not.

A useful alert policy evaluates at least four dimensions:

1. Residual exposure after recognized contingency
2. Persistence across forecast cycles
3. Time remaining to act
4. Business, contractual or regulatory consequence

The deterministic policy should classify MONITOR, REVIEW or ESCALATE. An LLM can explain the result and route it to the right audience—but it should not invent the policy.

The takeaway: a threshold should create a candidate signal, not make the final alert decision.
