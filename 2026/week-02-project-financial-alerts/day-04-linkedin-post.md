Day 4/5 — A recovery plan can improve one project and damage the portfolio

At 8:40, Project Meridian’s +$8M forecast variance has been validated, escalated and explained.

The agent now recommends:

“Move two shared architects from Project Beacon to Meridian for six weeks.”

On Meridian’s dashboard, the proposal looks excellent:

• EAC improves from $88M to $84M
• Forecast delay falls from 21 days to 8

But those architects are not spare capacity.

When the same proposal is viewed across the portfolio:

• Beacon’s EAC rises from $72M to $74.5M
• Beacon’s go-live slips by 18 days
• A $12M acceptance milestone moves into the next quarter
• Net portfolio EAC improves by only $1.5M—not $4M

Now compare a second option: phase Meridian’s scope and resequence its remaining work.

• Meridian EAC becomes $85.5M
• Its delay reduces to 12 days
• Beacon remains unchanged
• The $12M milestone stays in the current quarter
• Net portfolio EAC improves by $2.5M

The second option costs Meridian four days of recovery, but protects a dependent project and cash timing.

So the decision is no longer “Which option makes Meridian look best?”

It becomes “Which trade-off best fits the portfolio’s financial and strategic priorities?”

Before an agent recommends recovery, it should show at least four things:

1. Benefit to the affected project
2. Cost and schedule effects on dependent projects
3. Net portfolio cost and cash-timing impact
4. Capacity, priority and approval constraints

The takeaway: recovery plans are portfolio decisions disguised as project actions.
