Day 3/5 — We clarified the decision. Then we gave the AI agent the whole job.

By Wednesday, the invoice-exception decision was finally explicit.

The evidence was defined. The threshold was measurable. The decision owner was named. The escalation path was clear.

So leadership made the obvious leap:

“Good. Let the agent handle the rest.”

But “the rest” hides several different transfers of authority.

The agent can:

• gather the evidence
• analyse the exception
• recommend an action
• make the decision
• execute the decision
• monitor the outcome

Those activities do not need the same level of autonomy.

In our fictional case, the agent can collect the purchase order, receipt, invoice and supply-risk data. It can calculate the variance and recommend an override.

But the mismatch exceeds the authority threshold, payment release has a material financial consequence, and the exception is only partly reversible.

That decision still belongs to a human.

The agent may execute only after the approval is recorded—and then monitor the result.

This is the idea I am testing today:

Autonomy is not a switch. It is an envelope.

For every stage of work, ask:

• What is the potential impact?
• Can the action be reversed?
• Is the required evidence complete?
• Does policy cover this situation?
• Is the exception familiar or novel?

The answer may be different at every stage.

“Human in the loop” is too vague. Which human? At which point? Reviewing what? With what authority—and enough time to intervene?
