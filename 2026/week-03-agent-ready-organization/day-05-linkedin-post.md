Day 5/5 — A practical way to start an AI-agent pilot

I started this week with a hypothesis:

AI-agent adoption may succeed more often when roles, responsibilities and decision rights are clear.

After four experiments, I would refine it.

The requirement is not perfect documentation. It is work that people and agents can understand—and adapt as evidence and conditions change.

Our fictional test case was simple:

An $84,600 invoice.
A 6.2% mismatch.
A supplier warning that shipments may stop within 48 hours.

Yet it exposed four different problems.

Day 1: One vague instruction produced three reasonable answers.

Day 2: A RACI named participants, but not the decision.

Day 3: A clear decision did not mean the agent should own every stage.

Day 4: A capable recommendation failed when the organization could not act in time.

This left me with a much simpler pilot playbook:

1. Pick one decision—not an entire job.

For example: “Should this invoice be held, paid or escalated?”

2. Explain that decision in five lines.

What starts it? What must be checked? Who says yes? What may the agent do? When must it stop?

3. Divide the moves.

For each move—find, check, suggest, decide, act and watch—write Human, Agent or Together.

4. Set the stop rules.

Stale data? Missing owner? Policy gap? Irreversible action? Stop and escalate.

5. Rehearse a bad day.

What happens when the owner is away, the evidence is wrong or the agent makes a bad recommendation? Can the team pause, correct and recover?

6. Let the pilot earn more trust.

Did the decision become faster or better? Did the agent escalate at the right time? Could people override and recover? Did the business outcome improve?

Only expand the agent’s authority when the evidence supports the next move.

The biggest takeaway for me:

AI agents do not simply enter software architectures.

They enter human operating systems.

Organization design is part of the system the agent runs inside.

And clarity does not mean rigidity.

The decision, stop rules and escalation path should change with the work. The goal is not to automate today’s org chart, but to connect human judgment and machine capability.

So before asking, “Which jobs can agents replace?”

I would start with:

“Which decisions can we explain well enough for humans and agents to improve together?”
