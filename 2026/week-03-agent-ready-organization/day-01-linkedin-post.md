Day 1/5 — Is the AI agent confused, or is the organization?

At 9:12 on Monday morning, an AI agent receives an invoice exception.

Invoice value: $84,600
Price mismatch: 6.2%
Supplier warning: shipments may stop in 48 hours

The instruction looks simple:

“Resolve material discrepancies with the appropriate approver.”

But the agent finds three perfectly plausible interpretations.

Accounts Payable says: hold the invoice until the records match.

Procurement says: protect supply; the buyer can accept a small commercial variance.

Operations says: release the payment now and correct it later.

So which action is correct?

The problem may not be the agent’s reasoning.

The organization has not made five things explicit:

• What outcome takes priority?
• Who owns the final decision?
• What does “material” mean?
• What may the agent actually do?
• Who owns the exception when policy and business continuity conflict?

Humans often absorb this ambiguity through experience, informal conversations, and knowing who “usually handles it.”

An AI agent makes that invisible operating model visible.

This leads to my working hypothesis for this week:

Organizations with clear, measurable, and adaptable definitions of work may have a higher probability of implementing AI agents successfully.

Not because clarity makes the model smarter.

Because it gives both humans and agents a shared way to understand ownership, authority, handoffs, exceptions, and success.
